The recording at a glance

Your copy is the 1984 PolyGram-era reissue Bird On Verve, Volume 3 (With Strings), catalog 817 444-1, part of a numbered series that reissued Parker's Granz recordings. Its eight string tracks come from one afternoon: July 5, 1950, in New York. The cover is David Stone Martin's "two birds" drawing from the original 1950 Mercury release of the same session.

The hook

This record catches bebop's most radical musician doing the thing his fans least expected: playing love songs over violins, a harp and a French horn, because he wanted to.

Parker's interest in recording with strings dated back to 1941, and when he signed with Norman Granz he asked for it. The first strings session (November 1949) produced "Just Friends," the biggest-selling record of his life. This LP is the sequel, cut seven months later with a new arranger, Joe Lipman, whom Parker preferred.

The twist on side A is the two takes of "Relaxin' With Lee," recorded just four weeks earlier. That June 6, 1950 date was Parker and Dizzy Gillespie's last studio session together and Parker's only studio meeting with Thelonious Monk. So one side of this $20 record holds both faces of Bird in the summer of 1950: the bebop architect with his oldest ally, and the would-be concert artist who told Nat Hentoff that Bartók had become his favorite composer.

Critics called the strings a sellout. Parker answered that he was looking for new ways of saying things musically.

The music

Nine of the ten tracks are Broadway and Hollywood songs from 1931 to 1944, the "Great American Songbook" (the shared stock of popular songs jazz players use as raw material). According to Lipman, Parker picked every song himself. Almost all follow the standard 32-bar song form: four 8-bar sections, usually AABA (a tune, the tune again, a contrasting "bridge," the tune once more). Each track is about three minutes because each was cut to fit one side of a 10-inch 78 rpm disc.

Open any track for its story and what to listen for.

Side A

  1. The story

    A Parker original from the June 6, 1950 quintet date, taken at a relaxed medium tempo next to the session's faster burners like "Bloomdido" and "Leap Frog." Bop players often wrote new melodies over the chords of older songs (called a "contrafact"); jazz discussion forums say this one sits on the chords of the 1934 swing hit "Stompin' at the Savoy," though I could not confirm that in a primary source. It is the only small-group bebop on the record, and it lets you hear how much tighter and more spare Parker sounds with a pianist like Monk than with a string section.

    Listen for

    Parker and Gillespie play the opening theme in unison, then trade solos. Strain to hear Monk under them: his chords are sparse, oddly placed jabs instead of a steady comp (accompaniment pattern). Two versions back to back let you hear that Parker never plays a solo the same way twice.

  2. The story

    The second of the two versions on this LP. Neither timing matches the issued master (about 2:47), so these are most likely alternate or edited takes from June 6, 1950.

    Listen for

    Compare it phrase by phrase with A1. The tune is the same; Parker's solo is not.

  3. The story

    Written for the Broadway revue The Band Wagon, it is a brooding minor-tinged ballad about two people moving through uncertainty together. Lipman frames it with lush string pads, and Parker states the melody almost straight before slipping into his own lines.

    Listen for

    Notice how close Parker stays to the written melody at first. That is the strings-era Bird: decorating rather than demolishing.

  4. The story

    A Bing Crosby hit and a jam-session favorite. Its chords move in a way improvisers love, sliding between keys a third apart, which is why so many bop tunes borrow them. Parker plays it with a light, conversational swing.

    Listen for

    The fastest-feeling ballad here. Listen for the moment the harmony jumps to an unexpected key early in each section; that jump is why jazz players love this tune.

  5. The story

    David Raksin wrote it as the theme for Otto Preminger's 1944 film noir Laura; Johnny Mercer added lyrics after the film's success. The melody never settles where you expect: it keeps moving through a chain of chords that step down by key (a "cycle of fifths" pattern) before it finally lands home, which gives it its haunted, searching quality. Parker plays it close to the melody, at under three minutes, and the restraint is the point. This track was not on the original U.S. 10-inch LP; it surfaced later, which is part of why reissues like yours matter.

    Listen for

    The highlight for slow, intense listening. The melody seems to keep reaching for a resting place and not finding it. Parker's long, held notes at phrase ends show off his tone more than anything else on the record.

Side B

  1. The story

    Written by Brooks Bowman, a Princeton student, for a campus musical show. It is a gentle, dreamy tune; Lipman's oboe and harp give it a fairy-tale glow that suits the title.

    Listen for

    Track the oboe and harp. Lipman uses them like a second voice answering Parker between phrases.

  2. The story

    One of George Gershwin's last songs, written for the Fred Astaire and Ginger Rogers film Shall We Dance. It is a love song about memory: you can lose the person, but not the way they hold a knife or sing off key. Parker swings this one harder than the others, and Bernie Leighton gets more of a role at the piano.

    Listen for

    The swingiest track. Listen for the piano getting more room, and for Parker's lines speeding up into double-time (twice as many notes per beat) for a few bars before relaxing.

  3. The story

    Cole Porter's ballad from the film Born to Dance, sung there by Jimmy Stewart. Its melody has long, arching phrases that reward a saxophonist's breath control. Parker floats on top of it.

    Listen for

    Listen to the long Porter phrases and how Parker breathes across them without breaking the line.

  4. The story

    Introduced in the 1935 film Every Night at Eight. It is probably the most familiar melody on the record, and you can hear Parker enjoying playing with it, bending the phrasing while keeping it recognizable.

    Listen for

    The tune you already know, so focus on how Parker shifts its rhythm: notes slightly early or late, phrases chopped or stretched, while the melody stays recognizable.

  5. The story

    First heard in the 1942 Abbott and Costello film Ride 'Em Cowboy, it became a bebop staple because its chords shift key abruptly between sections. It closes the record on an up-tempo note, with Parker ending on a lift rather than a sigh.

    Listen for

    Up-tempo closer. Listen for the sections where the key changes; Parker glides across them without a seam.

The composer and his world

The "composer" that matters here is Parker himself, since jazz treats the improviser as the author. In summer 1950 he was at the top of his fame and near the start of his collapse. Five years later he was dead at 34.

  4. 1950June 6: last studio date with Gillespie, only one with Monk. July 5: this strings session. Strings concerts at Birdland, the Apollo and Carnegie Hall.
  5. 1951Loses his New York cabaret card after a narcotics arrest.
  6. 1954Two suicide attempts; admitted to Bellevue.
  7. 1955Last gig at Birdland, March 5. Dies March 12.

Where Parker was. Born in Kansas City in 1920, he had spent the mid-1940s building bebop with Dizzy Gillespie, a fast, harmonically dense style that turned jazz from dance music into listening music. In late 1949 a Broadway club opened named Birdland in his honor, and he played opening night. He toured Europe in 1949 and 1950, signed with Granz, and in 1950 began living with Chan Richardson. These strings dates were his bid for a broader audience and for respect as a serious artist.

What came next. Within months the slide began. A narcotics arrest cost him his New York "cabaret card," the police permit then required to play in the city's clubs (sources date the revocation to 1951). Unable to work in his own namesake club, he toured outside the city, sank into debt, attempted suicide twice in 1954, and played his last gig at Birdland on March 5, 1955. He died a week later, on March 12, in Baroness Nica de Koenigswarter's suite at the Stanhope Hotel.

The currents of the time. In 1950 the record business was mid-revolution: 78s were giving way to the long-playing record, and magnetic tape had just replaced direct-to-disc recording. Pop music was dominated by lush orchestral ballads, and Billie Holiday had already shown in 1944 that strings could take a jazz voice to a wider audience. Meanwhile jazz critics were fiercely protective of bebop as an art music, which is why strings drew cries of "commercial."

The songwriters. The tunes come from the golden age of Tin Pan Alley, Broadway and Hollywood: George and Ira Gershwin, Cole Porter, Jimmy McHugh and Dorothy Fields, Arthur Schwartz and Howard Dietz, Johnny Green, and the film composer David Raksin. These were the pop hits of Parker's youth, and bebop players knew their chord changes cold. Earlier independent labels had avoided recording standards because of royalty costs; Granz's backing let Parker finally play the songs he loved.

The performers

Parker is the star, but the record's character comes from pairing a bebop soloist with New York's top commercial studio musicians.

Charlie "Bird" Parker

Alto saxophone. The most influential saxophonist in jazz, known for blinding speed, a piercing, unvibrato'd tone, and the ability to hear a dozen ways through every chord. On the strings records his solos are shorter and more melodic than on his small-group sides. One jazz historian argues that after "Just Friends" someone asked him to stick closer to the melody, and he obliged; that is very audible here.

Joe Lipman

Arranger and conductor. He started at 19 as pianist in Benny Goodman's orchestra and arranged for Goodman, Bunny Berigan (including Berigan's famous "I Can't Get Started"), Jimmy Dorsey, Sarah Vaughan and Billy Eckstine. Parker did not care much for the first arranger, Jimmy Carroll, and preferred Lipman for later dates; Lipman said Parker was easy to work with. Lipman also arranged the third strings session in January 1952. Analysts hear the 1950 date as closer to an orchestral record with a jazz soloist, where 1949 sounded more like a jazz group with strings added.

The rhythm section

Ray Brown was then the bassist of choice for Granz and soon the anchor of Oscar Peterson's trio; his walking lines are the floor everything stands on. Buddy Rich, the explosive big-band drummer, keeps unusually quiet here, serving the ballads. Bernie Leighton was one of the busiest studio pianists in New York, rarely heard as a soloist.

The strings

Five violins, a viola, a cello, harp, oboe and French horn, all classically trained session players led by concertmaster Sam Caplan. The oboe (Edwin Brown) and French horn (Joseph Singer) give Lipman's charts their color beyond plain violins.

On "Relaxin' With Lee"

Dizzy Gillespie co-invented bebop with Parker; this June 1950 date was their last studio session together. Thelonious Monk, bebop's great eccentric, was Parker's choice at piano, but reviewers note he is buried in the mix and gets little solo space. Curly Russell on bass and Buddy Rich again on drums complete the quintet.

The recording itself

Your LP is a 1984 reissue of mono tapes from 1950, wearing the logo of a label that did not exist until 1956.

The label trail. Norman Granz, the impresario behind the Jazz at the Philharmonic concerts, ran his Clef label in 1950 as an imprint pressed and distributed by Mercury Records; that is why these sessions first appeared on Mercury. Mitch Miller, then Mercury's head of A&R (artists and repertoire), helped organize the first strings date. Clef went independent in 1953, and in 1956 Granz folded Clef and Norgran into a new label, Verve. He sold Verve to MGM in 1960; MGM sold its record labels to Polydor in 1972, which brought Verve into PolyGram. By the 1980s PolyGram was mining the catalog with series like this one.

The release history. The July 1950 session first came out as a set of 78s and a 10-inch LP, Mercury MG C-509 (later MG C-109), which left off "Dancing in the Dark" and "Laura." In 1955 Clef combined both strings albums on one 12-inch LP (MG C-675), reissued by Verve in 1957 as April in Paris: The Genius of Charlie Parker #2. Your Bird On Verve series was a 1984 run of numbered LPs (Volume 2, 817 443-1, is Bird and Diz; Volume 7 is 817 448-1). Verve's 1995 CD Charlie Parker with Strings: The Master Takes later gathered all the strings masters.

The technology. The session was recorded in mono, early in the tape era. Each tune was built to fit one 78 rpm side, roughly three minutes. "Hi-Fi" on your cover is period branding the 1984 sleeve reproduces from 1950s Verve LPs, not a technical claim about this pressing. Expect a narrow but warm mono image: Parker front and center, strings blended behind him.

The cover. The art is by David Stone Martin (1913-1992), whose signature is at bottom right. He was Granz's longtime friend and in-house illustrator, designed the Jazz at the Philharmonic "trumpeter" logo, and drew more than 400 album covers, often in line art plus one or two flat colors, exactly as here. A collector's note: neither original strings 10-inch LP said "#2" on its cover, and the one with the two birds is the second. Your Volume 3 sleeve is that second cover.

Why it matters

The strings records were Parker's best sellers in his lifetime, were largely condemned by jazz purists, and were inducted into the Grammy Hall of Fame in 1988.

Then. Fans and critics accused Bird of going commercial. Parker pushed back that he was looking for new ways of saying things musically, and the evidence supports him: he initiated the project, chose the songs, took the string group on the road (Birdland and the Apollo in August 1950, Carnegie Hall that September, more dates through 1952), and reportedly played "Just Friends" for the doctor attending him in his final days.

Now. Critical opinion has softened into a split verdict. Most agree that Parker's playing is gorgeous and that the 1949 "Just Friends" is the peak, while the charts can sound stiff or sweet next to his small-group sides. Jazz Lines Publications has since published the original Carroll and Lipman scores from the session parts, and saxophonist Aaron Johnson staged a historically accurate recreation in New York in 2014.

Compared with other versions. This session competes mainly with its own sibling: the 1949 Carroll date ("Just Friends," "April in Paris," "Summertime") is the more famous and arguably more inspired. The live Midnight Jazz at Carnegie Hall (September 1950) shows the same ensemble with more fire.

Footprint. The concept launched a whole genre: Clifford Brown with Strings (1955) and Stan Getz's Focus (1961) both follow the template. And the album's David Stone Martin covers remain some of the most reproduced images in jazz design.

How to listen

Play side B first, then side A: side B is pure strings session, and hearing "Relaxin' With Lee" after it makes the contrast hit harder.

On every ballad, listen for three layers: the string "pad" (sustained chords), the rhythm section underneath (Ray Brown's bass is the easiest to track), and Parker on top.

A general map for each string track. Most follow the same shape: a short orchestral introduction; Parker plays the melody once through, lightly decorated; a brief solo stretch where he leaves the melody; then the strings or the bridge bring him back to the tune, and a short ending (often a held chord with a harp sweep). Count along in 8-bar chunks and you can find the bridge each time: it is where the harmony suddenly moves somewhere new.

Track-by-track notes live in the tracklist under The music; open any song and read "Listen for."

Piano connections

These songs use the same building blocks you are practicing in Alfred's. The "dominant seventh" chord you have been fingering (D7, for example) is the engine of every one of these tunes, pulling toward the next chord. The AABA form is a longer cousin of the repeated-section pieces in your method book.

"Relaxin' With Lee" is not a 12-bar blues like "Got Those Blues!", but the bebop way of playing over it uses the same swing feel. And the chain of chords stepping down in "Laura" is related to the repeating chord pattern you are hearing in Canon in D, where the bass keeps walking down and the harmony follows.
