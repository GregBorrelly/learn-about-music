Three catches

  • The cover bills Fanny first, but the program runs Clara first.
  • The shell and inlay, as read earlier, call Fanny's trio "D major." It is in D minor, and a later US club edition repeats the same slip.
  • BBC Music Magazine's "1988" is the session year, not the release year.

The hook

This tape pairs two trios that were nearly a conversation, and it leaves you to decide which verdict to believe: the composer's or the music's.

Clara Schumann finished hers in September 1846. In March 1847 she was visiting Fanny Hensel's Berlin household almost daily, while Fanny was struggling with a trio of her own. Fanny played its premiere on April 11 and was dead on May 14. Clara had reportedly meant to dedicate her trio to Fanny, and published it that year with no dedication at all.

Then comes the twist. Once Robert Schumann wrote his own trio, Clara said she no longer cared for hers, calling it effeminate and sentimental by comparison. Scholar Nicole Grimes now argues it was a formal model for Robert's trio, and for Brahms and Smetana after him. So the woman who dismissed the piece may have taught her husband how to write his.

The music

Both works are the only piano trios their composers wrote, both are four-movement, minor-key pieces, and both were written by pianists who had to think hard about the two strings. A piano trio is piano, violin and cello; by the 1840s it often ran like a small symphony. Open any movement for its story and what to listen for.

L

Clara Schumann

Piano Trio in G minor, Op. 17 (1846)

Why it exists. Clara wrote it in Dresden between May and September 1846, after a winter studying Bach and Cherubini counterpoint (music built from independent melodies) with Robert. Her son Emil, born that February, was very ill. She suffered what sources describe as a likely miscarriage on the summer trip to Norderney, and gave a concert the next day. It came after her Op. 16 preludes and fugues, which critics had found surprising from a "feminine mind."

Premiere and reception. She premiered it in Vienna on January 15, 1847. Viennese reviewers praised its clarity, while still expressing surprise at such control from a woman. Breitkopf & Härtel published it that year. Mendelssohn and Joachim admired the finale's fugal passage.

  1. The story

    Sonata form: an opening theme, a contrasting theme, a development, a return. The violin states the first theme alone before the piano takes it.

    Listen for

    The violin opens alone with the first theme, then the piano takes it over. A chordal second theme arrives in the piano. In the development, listen for violin and cello tangling in independent lines. When the opening returns, both themes stay in G minor, so the bright second theme now sounds darker.

  2. The story

    A dance at minuet speed with a snappy dotted rhythm. The cello plucks under the violin while the piano adds chords.

    Listen for

    Count it in three. Catch the short-long snap in the tune and the plucked cello beneath. The middle Trio is smoother; then the opening returns.

  3. The story

    Eight bars of solo piano, then the violin takes the song. The middle turns restless and minor before the calm returns.

    Listen for

    The emotional center. Eight bars of piano alone, in major, then the violin takes the song. Mark the moment all three lock into dotted long-short rhythms and the mood turns restless.

  4. The story

    Sonata form again, with the opening theme treated as a fugue subject in the development and coda.

    Listen for

    Listen for the theme turning into a fugue subject, passed between violin and cello as the piano runs elaborately around them. Sources differ on the opening: the Open University hears the Andante's theme in it, Wikipedia the first movement's. Decide for yourself.

What makes it distinctive. Clara had studied violin as a girl, and it shows: the strings get real themes rather than decoration. Grimes hears the fugal writing as a compositional virtuosity that matches the piano part.

R

Fanny Mendelssohn

Piano Trio in D minor, Op. 11 (1847)

Why it exists. It was a birthday present for her sister Rebecka. Sources disagree on the start date: Wikipedia, citing R. Larry Todd, says 1846-47, while All Classical, citing Grove, says early 1847. In March 1847 she wrote in her diary that the trio was giving her a lot of trouble.

Premiere and reception. Fanny played the piano on April 11, 1847 at her Sunday concert series, with diplomat Robert von Keudell on violin and her brother Paul on cello. A Berlin critic that year called the first movement a masterpiece. Days after the premiere she worried in her diary that her inspiration was going. She finished one more piece, a song, and died May 14. Breitkopf & Härtel published the trio in 1850.

  1. The story

    Starts softly but with fierce energy, driven by whirling piano figures, then opens into a lyrical second theme.

    Listen for

    The piano churns from the first bar while the strings sing a broad, yearning tune above it. Memorize the lyrical second theme; it is the payoff at the very end. Ask whether the strings get drowned, since balance is the hard problem here.

  2. The story

    A delicate piano solo opens it, like one of Felix's Songs without Words.

    Listen for

    Piano alone first. Notice how naturally the strings enter a texture that began as a solo piece.

  3. The story

    A song where you would expect a scherzo, with a prominent reference to an aria from Felix's oratorio Elijah.

    Listen for

    It lasts only a couple of minutes. Listen for the echo of Elijah, Felix's recent oratorio, inside his sister's song.

  4. The story

    An almost improvised piano solo, drama, and then the return of the first movement's lyrical theme.

    Listen for

    A long piano introduction, almost improvised, as she might have played at her own concerts. Wait for the first movement's second theme to come back.

What makes it distinctive. The piano part is demanding, the mood is angrier than you might expect from a birthday gift, and the song keeps breaking in. Sheila Hayman, her descendant, calls it her masterpiece.

The composers and their world

In 1846-47 both women were celebrated keyboard players whose composing was treated as a sideline, and they knew each other when they wrote these trios.

Clara Schumann (1819-1896). She was trained by her father, Friedrich Wieck, from age five, played the Leipzig Gewandhaus at nine, and caused such a stir in Vienna in 1838 that police were called to the box office. Her marriage to Robert in 1840 took a lawsuit against her father. By 1846 she was keeping a family afloat in Dresden, touring and teaching while Robert struggled with illness. Her confidence was fragile: in 1839 she had written that no woman had yet managed to compose, and asked why she should expect to be the first. After Robert's death in 1856 she mostly stopped composing and spent four more decades as one of Europe's leading pianists, supporting her children.

Fanny Mendelssohn Hensel (1805-1847). She had the same education as her brother Felix until the point it mattered. Anna Beer tells of their father returning from a trip when she was 14: Felix got a notebook for his first opera, and she got jewels and a message that music would be an ornament to her home. Felix wrote in 1837 that publishing would only disturb her household duties. He also published six of her songs under his own name, and Queen Victoria picked one of hers as her favorite of his. Her husband, the painter Wilhelm Hensel, reportedly left blank manuscript paper on her music stand each morning. She ran the family's Sunday concerts in Berlin and wrote over 450 works. Only in 1846 did she publish under her own name.

The end of the story. Fanny had a stroke while rehearsing Felix's Die erste Walpurgisnacht and died at 41. Felix died of the same cause that November, after writing his F minor quartet in her memory. Clara's son Emil died that June. Robert's first trio, Op. 63, appeared the same year, and Clara heard her own differently afterward.

The currents of the time. Berlin had food riots and political unrest the spring Fanny's trio was heard, a year before the 1848 revolutions. Women could perform in public, but publishing large works was almost exclusively a male domain. For a woman of Fanny's class, a professional career counted as indecent. Counterpoint was the sharp edge of this: writing fugues was a claim to the craft people assumed women lacked.

What came next. Both trios were played in the 19th century and then faded. Fanny's music stayed largely out of reach until late in the 20th century; most of it was hard to access until the 1990s, which is why a tape from 1989 sits near the start of the revival. In 2010 Angela Mace Christian proved Fanny, not Felix, wrote the Easter Sonata.

The performers

The Dartington Piano Trio was a working English group rooted in teaching and regional concerts, not a star ensemble.

The ensemble. Hyperion says it formed in 1980 from the larger Dartington Ensemble, continuing the public music-making of the Dartington String Quartet. Its concerts included many schools in South Devon, and it recorded Martinů, Bridge and this program for Hyperion. Dartington is the arts estate in Devon, home to a college of arts and a famous summer music school. By a 1998 recording the trio had a different pianist, John Bryden, so this tape captures one lineup. I found no dates for the group's end.

Where this sits in their careers. It is one of a small Hyperion catalog that made the trio's name beyond Devon. No member's biography treats it as a landmark, but it is the trio's lasting calling card: WNYC was still playing the Fanny in April 2026.

The recording itself

This tape comes from the decade when a label started on minicab money became the place to find music nobody else would record.

The label. Ted Perry founded Hyperion in 1980, driving a minicab at night while his family packed records. Its first release won over clarinetist Thea King with a cow on the cover, her favorite animal. Perry then heard Hildegard of Bingen on BBC Radio 3 while driving late at night, and the 1982 result, A Feather on the Breath of God, paid for his mistakes, as he later joked. His stated aim was records no one else would make. In 1988-89, two trios by women fit that brief exactly.

This release. KA appears to be the cassette prefix for CDA66331. The program moved to the budget Helios line in 2001 as CDH55078, and to a 2008 Musical Heritage Society club CD. Helios, named for Hyperion's mythical son, has since been wound down. Hyperion resisted streaming until Universal bought it in 2023.

Credits. The club-edition listing names Antony Howell as engineer and Terry Shannon as designer. Those names, with Cecile Kelly and Edward Perry, recur on other Hyperion discs, so they look like the house team. A discography marks Kelly and Perry "Epr," and Ted Perry's full name was George Edward Perry; that he is this Edward Perry is my inference. I could not verify the producer or the venue. Hyperion's own booklet (PDF 55078-B, by Peter Lamb) blocked my request, so check the inlay.

The technology. The catalog entry describes the CD as digital stereo, which tells you about the disc, not necessarily the session. The cassette is an analog copy of that program, made just as enthusiasm for the CD was peaking. From an earlier reading of the shell, it is chrome (Type II) with Dolby, the type unstated. Set a deck to chrome and try Dolby B first; if it sounds dull, switch it off.

The cover. A photograph of orange-red maple leaves, backlit against a blue sky. It suits the Hyperion habit of pictures over performer portraits. I could not verify the photographer.

Why it matters

This is an early, serious pairing of two works now central to the rediscovery of women composers, made just before the scholarship caught up.

Then. Neither trio was unknown to record collectors. The Clara had a likely first recording on Decca in 1951 and the Beaux Arts Trio's in 1972; the Macalester Trio recorded both for a Vox box in the late 1970s. What Hyperion offered was the two together as their own album, from a respected label. As late as 1979, a scholar had no catalog to work from and depended on the archive's director for access to Fanny's papers in Berlin, and a keeper of those papers dismissed her as just a housewife.

Reviews. I found no contemporary 1989-90 review. BBC Music Magazine's Martin Cotton gave the Helios reissue four stars while calling both composers somewhat mechanical, and rating Fanny the more interesting. He does not discuss the Dartington's playing. Hyperion's own label calls the trio's recordings highly acclaimed, which is not independent evidence.

Where critics disagree. Cotton hears Clara as less enterprising; Grimes hears a formal innovator who shaped Robert and Brahms. MusicWeb finds Clara's writing confident and Fanny's bold and forceful. As Grimes puts it, Clara's own self-doubt cast a long shadow over the piece.

Against other recordings. The direct rival is Hyperion's own 2019 remake with the Nash Ensemble, praised by The Strad for its song-like Andante and turbulent opening to the Fanny, and adding Fanny's string quartet. Anne-Sophie Mutter brought the Clara to Sony in 2022. The Dartington's advantage now is historical: it shows how these works sounded when they were still news.

Cultural footprint. Clara's first movement is a set work on the Edexcel A-level syllabus in England, with the LSO teaching it. Fanny's story reached cinemas in the 2023 documentary Fanny: The Other Mendelssohn. I found no film, TV or ballpark use of either trio.

How to listen

Play Clara straight through, then Fanny, and ask one question of both: how does a great pianist share the room with two strings?

Connections to the piano

  • Three keys on the U1. Play a G minor scale, then G major, then B-flat major. Clara's movements live in those three moods. B-flat is G minor's relative major (same key signature), and G major is its parallel major (same home note).
  • The D7. The D7 chord from Alfred's is the chord that pulls home to G. In G minor it is the engine of Clara's cadences, the musical punctuation at the ends of phrases.
  • Bach. The Menuet in G from the Anna Magdalena book is a dance in three, like Clara's Scherzo. Her fugal writing comes from the same two-independent-lines idea, grown larger.
  • Melody over accompaniment. Fanny's slow-movement openings use the layout from Alfred's: tune in one hand, flowing support in the other, only far richer.

Pressing one turns sound on and plays a short synthesized arpeggio. It is a key, not the music.
